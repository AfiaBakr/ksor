A feasibility study for a construction project in Pakistan has to clear four
gates, roughly in this order, before a design is worth committing money to.

## 1. Land-use and zoning feasibility

Confirm the site's permitted use against the relevant area authority's master
plan / zoning classification — residential, commercial, industrial, or a
mixed category — **before** any design work. Every area document in this
section names the authority that holds this master plan. A use that is not
permitted on the plot cannot be approved regardless of design quality.

## 2. Regulatory approval feasibility

Identify which authority's building bye-laws govern the site (see the area
documents), what NOCs are required (fire, environment, civil aviation height
clearance near airports, etc. — these vary by area and are not enumerated
here), and the authority's plan-approval and completion-certificate process.

## 3. Technical / structural feasibility

Design must comply with the applicable **Building Code of Pakistan** seismic
provisions (see
[the overview](overview.md#the-three-tracks-that-run-in-parallel-everywhere))
and be executed by PEC-registered engineers and PCATP-registered architects,
since only registered professionals and firms may lawfully offer these
services.[^pec-fee-structures]

## 4. Financial feasibility — the estimation instruments

Pakistan uses **two distinct, non-interchangeable** rate instruments. Confusing
them is a common estimation error.

> [!WARNING]
> The **Composite Schedule of Rates (CSR)** estimates what it costs to
> **build**. The **FBR valuation table / DC rate** estimates what a property
> is **worth** for tax and stamp-duty purposes. Neither substitutes for the
> other, and neither should be used as a stand-in for a contractor's own bid.

### Composite Schedule of Rates (CSR) — construction cost estimation

Each province (and AJK) publishes its own CSR, prepared by its Communication
& Works / Planning & Development department, giving unit rates for
manpower, materials and equipment across its districts. A CSR-based estimate
applies these unit rates to quantities taken off the drawings (a bill of
quantities), then adds a **25% markup on the estimated unit cost to cover
overheads, taxes, preliminaries and profit** — this markup figure is stated
in the CSR methodology itself, not a general industry rule of
thumb.[^csr-methodology] CSR editions are dated and revised (2025 editions
exist for Punjab, Sindh and KP, for example) — always use the **current**
edition for the province of the site, never a carried-forward
figure.[^csr-punjab-2025][^csr-sindh-2025][^csr-kp-2025][^csr-balochistan][^csr-ajk]

### FBR valuation tables and DC rates — property valuation, not construction cost

The **FBR valuation table** is the Federal Board of Revenue's official
per-area value for a property, used to compute capital gains tax and
withholding tax on property transactions; it is published and periodically
revised (most recently, per this record's research, city-wise updates dated 1
November 2024) on FBR's own site.[^fbr-valuation] The **DC rate** ("collector
rate") is set separately by the district administration and used for stamp
duty and registration fee on transfer — it is a different, usually lower,
figure from the FBR rate, and both are administrative valuations rather than
market price.

### Professional consultancy fees

The **Pakistan Engineering Council** publishes fee structures for firm and
individual registration/renewal — figures found in this research ranged
roughly Rs. 10,000–30,000 per year depending on category — but this record has
**not** located PEC's schedule of consultancy-service fees (commonly expressed
as a percentage of project cost, banded by project type), nor PCATP's scale of
professional charges for architects.[^pec-fee-structures][^pcatp-fee]

> Open question: the current PEC "Schedule of Fees for Consultancy Services"
> and PCATP's scale of professional charges are the authoritative source for
